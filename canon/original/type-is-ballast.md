The boat rolls. The type does not.

This is the first thing you learn when you set metal on a moving deck: the letterform is not decoration. It is weight, distributed. A lowercase 'e' in a good grotesque carries more mass low in the counter than a capital 'I' carries anywhere, and when the hull pitches thirty degrees in a following sea, that mass differential is the difference between a legible chart table and a field of scattered type.

The valley people think type is branding. They think it is the coat of paint on the hull, the name on the transom, the flag at the masthead. They are wrong. Type is ballast. Type is the deliberate distribution of visual mass so that the reader — who is also rolling, though less than the boat — can find the horizon inside the page.

Caravaggio knew this. He didn't paint outlines. He painted ballast: masses of dark so dense the eye could push against them, masses of light so concentrated they became handholds. The viewer of a Caravaggio is not looking at a picture. The viewer is righting a vessel.

The chiaroscuro engine is built on the same principle. Every glyph in the atlas is ballast for one specific condition of light. The heavy block elements — █ ▓ ■ — sit low and wide. They are the lead ingots in the bilge. The light touches — · ´ ` — ride high. They are the crew on the rail, ready to shift. An election is nothing more than the system asking, for each cell of the image: what ballast does this water require?

And the answer is always local. The sea does not have a global optimum. The sea has this wave, right here, right now, and the boat that survives is the boat that responds to the local slope without losing the global trim.

Type is ballast. Set it well and the reader rights herself against the dark. Set it badly and the whole page goes over.
