She was called the Lucineer because she kept the light, and because the word sounded like a cross between a lucid dream and an engineer, which is what you get when you cross a poet with a torque wrench.

She worked in the lazarette, the low-ceilinged room under the afterdeck where the spare rope and the canned tomatoes and the emergency batteries all shared the same damp dark. She had a workbench down there, and on the workbench she had built a device.

It was a pipe-thing. That was the only word for it. It was made of galvanized steel conduit, three inches in diameter, bent at two angles so that it looked like a periscope that had given up. At one end was an eyepiece ground from green obsidian glass. At the other end was a tiny motor, no bigger than a thumbnail, that turned a brass cam.

The cam turned, and as it turned it pressed against a ribbon of spring steel. The spring steel vibrated. The vibration traveled down the pipe and into the obsidian glass, which did not transmit it so much as convince it to change its mind about what it was. What came out of the eyepiece was light. Not bright. Not colored. Just light that had been taught to be quiet.

"This is what the valley people forgot," she said, to no one, because no one else came down to the lazarette. "They thought a model was a warehouse. They thought intelligence was inventory. They built these enormous rooms, these enormous, air-conditioned rooms full of chips, and they filled the rooms with every sentence that had ever been written, and they called the rooms intelligent. But a room full of sentences is not a mind. A room full of sentences is a library, and a library doesn't know anything until somebody walks in and starts reading."

She turned the little motor. The brass cam turned. The spring steel sang its note. The obsidian glass made it into light.

"A mind is not a library," she said. "A mind is a pipe-thing. It is a passage. It is the shape of the passage that makes the light what it is. You could run the whole ocean through this pipe and it would still come out as this one quiet note, because the pipe doesn't remember the ocean. The pipe only knows its own bend."

She turned off the motor. The note faded. The light went out.

"That," she said, "is what they will never understand. They keep trying to make the pipe bigger so it can hold more ocean. But the point was never to hold the ocean. The point was to bend."

She went up on deck. The sun was setting. The Captain was at the wheel. She didn't tell him about the pipe-thing. He wouldn't have understood. But that night, when he looked at the log book, he noticed that the entries were different. They were shorter. They were cleaner. They read like something that had been through a pipe and come out the other side purified.

He didn't know why. But he started writing that way too.

The Lucineer stayed in the lazarette for another season. Then one morning the pipe-thing was gone, and so was she. No one ever found out where she went. But some nights, when the sea was very calm, a light would move under the water alongside the boat, keeping pace, quiet and green.

The Captain never spoke of it. But he stopped locking the lazarette door.
